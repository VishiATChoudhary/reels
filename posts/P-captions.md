## Tech-P02-Jobs-Arc

Steve Jobs found the answer in a study about birds.

Jobs once read a Scientific American study that ranked animals by how much energy they burn to move a kilometre. The condor came out on top. Humans, "the crown of creation", landed somewhere in the middle of the list.

Then someone measured a human on a bicycle. It beat the condor and went off the top of the chart.

That was how Jobs explained what a computer is: not a smarter brain, a tool that multiplies the one you have. "A bicycle for our minds."

Steve Jobs, Memory & Imagination, 1990.

## Tech-P03-Feynman

Feynman explained AI in 1985 with a cheetah.

At a lecture in 1985, Richard Feynman was asked whether machines would ever think like people.

His answer: we never built a machine that runs like a cheetah, we built wheels. Airplanes don't flap like birds, they still fly. So machines won't think the way we do, but on some tasks they'll do it better, faster, and differently.

Forty years later that is a pretty good description of the AI we actually got.

Richard Feynman, Esalen Institute, 1985.

## Tech-P04-Selfridge

In 1961 an MIT scientist made a promise about machines.

For a TV special on MIT's centenary, Oliver Selfridge, one of the founders of AI research, was asked whether computers could think.

He said we'd always be able to tell a man from a robot, and his daughter would never marry a computer. But machines "can and will think in our lifetime", and he expected something close to the robots of science fiction within 10 or 15 years.

He was off by about five decades. The direction was right.

Oliver Selfridge, The Thinking Machine, 1961.

## Tech-P05-Clarke

In 1964 Arthur C. Clarke described how you'd work.

On the BBC's Horizon, the author of 2001: A Space Odyssey said that within about fifty years you could run a business from Tahiti or Bali as well as from London, and surgeons in Edinburgh could operate on patients in New Zealand.

"Men will no longer commute, they will communicate."

The first transatlantic remote surgery happened in 2001. Remote work went mainstream in 2020, 56 years after this broadcast.

Arthur C. Clarke, BBC Horizon, 1964.

## Tech-P06-Hinton

The godfather of AI was asked one question he couldn't answer.

In 2023 Geoffrey Hinton quit Google so he could talk freely about the technology he spent fifty years building. On 60 Minutes he was asked how we keep it safe.

His answer: "I can't see a path that guarantees safety." The first time we deal with something totally new we usually get it wrong, and with this we can't afford to.

A year later he won the Nobel Prize in Physics for the work that made modern AI possible.

Geoffrey Hinton, 60 Minutes, CBS, 2023.

## Tech-P07-Move37

The move every Go expert called a mistake.

Seoul, March 2016. Game 2 of AlphaGo vs Lee Sedol, one of the best Go players alive. On move 37 the AI played a stone no professional would ever play.

The commentators thought it was a mistake. Fan Hui, the European champion who had lost to AlphaGo months earlier, was in the room: "normally, human, we never play this one, because it's bad."

Then the board came together. Every stone played before started working with that one. AlphaGo won the game, and the match 4 to 1. Fan Hui's verdict on move 37: "Maybe it's beautiful."

AlphaGo vs Lee Sedol, game 2, 2016.

## Tech-P08-Karpathy

The hottest new programming language isn't Python.

Andrej Karpathy (OpenAI founding member, former head of AI at Tesla) told a room of founders that software barely changed for 70 years, then changed twice in a few.

Software 1.0 is code you write. Software 2.0 is neural networks, trained, not written. Software 3.0 is now: large language models you program with prompts.

And those prompts are written in plain English. "Remarkably, we're now programming computers in English."

Andrej Karpathy, YC AI Startup School, 2025.

## Tech-P09-Hassabis

One protein used to cost a scientist five years.

The rule of thumb in biology: one PhD student, four or five years, to work out the structure of one protein. There are 200 million known to science.

DeepMind's AlphaFold learned to predict a structure in seconds. So they folded all of them. Demis Hassabis calls it "a billion years of PhD time done in one year."

In 2024 he shared the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for it. His next target: "maybe we can cure all disease with the help of AI."

Demis Hassabis, Cambridge lecture, with AlphaFold footage.

## Tech-P12-Hopper

Grace Hopper banned one phrase from her office.

Rear Admiral Grace Hopper, the computing pioneer behind the first compilers and COBOL, kept a clock in her office that ran counterclockwise. On day one, people couldn't tell the time. By day three, they realised there was never a reason clocks had to run clockwise.

The point: the most dangerous phrase in the language is "we've always done it that way."

And her promise to the audience: say it in the next 12 months and she will materialise beside you and haunt you for 24 hours.

Grace Hopper, NSA lecture, 1982.

## Tech-P13-Spacex

Elon Musk had money for just one more launch.

SpaceX's first rocket, Falcon 1, failed three times in a row between 2006 and 2008. Explosions, debris, a lot of people saying a private company couldn't reach orbit.

Musk later told 60 Minutes: "If that fourth launch hadn't worked, that would have been it. We would have not had the resources to mount a fifth."

On 28 September 2008 the fourth Falcon 1 made it to orbit, the first privately developed liquid-fuel rocket to do it. That night he told his team: "There's only a handful of countries on Earth that have done it. It's normally a country thing, not a company thing."

Elon Musk, 60 Minutes, CBS, 2012, and his speech to SpaceX staff, 2008.

## Tech-P14-Netflix

Blockbuster could have bought Netflix for $50 million.

Blockbuster made six billion dollars a year. Netflix, mailing DVDs in red envelopes, was nothing to them. So Marc Randolph and Reed Hastings flew to Dallas and offered to sell.

## Tech-P15-Airbnb

Brian Chesky's terrible idea was saved by cereal.

Strangers sleeping in other strangers' homes was "pretty universally considered a terrible idea." By late 2008 Airbnb had launched three times and nobody was using it. Chesky was hot gluing cereal boxes in his kitchen: Obama O's and Cap'n McCain's, made for the election.

At their Y Combinator interview, his roommate Joe Gebbia handed Paul Graham a box. Graham's reaction: if you can get people to pay $40 for a four dollar box of cereal, maybe you can convince them to sleep in each other's homes.

Y Combinator let them in. Airbnb went public in 2020.

Brian Chesky, The David Rubenstein Show, Bloomberg, 2022.

## Tech-P16-Bezos

Jeff Bezos quit his job by pretending to be 80.

In spring 1994 Bezos was a 30 year old senior vice president at D. E. Shaw, a Wall Street hedge fund. Then he read that web usage was growing 2,300 percent a year. "Things just don't grow that fast."

He told his boss, David Shaw, he was going to start a company selling books online. They took a two hour walk in Central Park. Shaw's verdict: a really good idea, but a better idea for somebody who didn't already have a good job. He asked Bezos to think about it for 48 hours.

So Bezos imagined himself at 80, looking back. He knew he wouldn't regret trying and failing. The one thing he might regret was never having tried. He left mid-year, walked away from his bonus, and started Amazon in a garage in Bellevue, Washington.

Jeff Bezos, Academy of Achievement interview, 4 May 2001.

## Tech-P17-Berners-Lee

Tim Berners-Lee's boss wrote three words on the proposal for the web.

In March 1989 Berners-Lee, a software engineer at CERN, wrote a memo called "Information Management: A Proposal": a global hypertext system to link the lab's scattered documents. CERN had committees for physics experiments. This wasn't one, so nobody quite knew where it should go, and not much happened to it.

His boss, Mike Sendall, wrote on the cover in pencil: "Vague but exciting..." In 1990 Sendall let him do it on the side as a play project, on a new NeXT computer. By the end of 1990 the first web server and browser were running on that NeXT.

On 30 April 1993 CERN put the web software in the public domain. No patent, no royalties, free for anyone to use.

Tim Berners-Lee, TED 2009 (The next web) and CERN's 30th anniversary of the web, 12 March 2019.

## Tech-P18-Dyson

James Dyson built 5,127 vacuums to beat one bag.

In the late 1970s his vacuum kept losing suction. He emptied the bag and it still wouldn't pick up. The bag wasn't full, its pores were clogged with the first dust that went in.

He had seen cyclones on factory and sawmill roofs, spinning dust out of the air without a filter. So he built a cardboard one onto his vacuum, then spent about five years in his coach house making one change at a time. 5,126 failures. Prototype 5,127 worked.

The big manufacturers looked at it and passed. Replacement bags were too good a business. So he borrowed against his house, set himself up as a manufacturer and launched the DC01 in 1993.

James Dyson, Masters of Scale with Reid Hoffman, 2024.
